* Determinism: attractive or repulsive? 
  - Some philosophers have been repulsed by determinism because it
    would seem to preclude human freedom.
  - Other philosophers have been attracted by determinism because it
    would seem to make our world intelligible, predictable, and
    perhaps even controllable.

* Philosophical resistance to natural philosophy
  - Compatibilism might actually be true. But I suspect that it is
    also motivated by a philosophical division of labor that we ---
    pursuers of natural philosophy --- would reject.

* Belot's false dilemma

"How do we decide whether or not a physical theory is deterministic?
You can find two sorts of answer to this question in contemporary
philosophical discussions. The older one is an inheritance from
logical empiricism: it makes determinism a formal property of theories
considered as linguistic entities. The more recent one is unabashedly
metaphysical: it takes determinism to be a matter of possible futures,
and thus involves us in modal metaphysics.

My purpose here is to convince you that there are good reasons for
favouring the more recent view: I argue that you cannot decide whether
or not a theory is deterministic without knowing something about the
interpretation of the theory. Thus determin- ism cannot be a formal
property of theories. On the other hand, providing an interpretation
for a theory naturally involves dealing with possibilia." (Determinism
and ontology, p 85)

"This completes the argument: determinism cannot be a formal property
of theories, because the same theory may be deterministic or
indeterministic, depending on how it is interpreted." (p 88)

* Interpretation is just more formalism

- A possible worlds analysis /is/ a formal analysis.
- An /interpretation/ of a theory includes a specification of identity
  criteria between models.

* Determinism: from material to formal mode of speech

* Possible worlds analyses

  - GTR (interpreted literally) might commit us to the existence of
    spacetime points, but surely it doesn't commit us to modal
    realism.

  - The development of semantics and model theory changed the
    standards of philosophical analysis.
  - In some cases, it was not just the analysans that invoked new
    concepts (sets, worlds, ,etc), but the analysandum itself was
    changed.
    - For example, instead of completing "a theory T is deterministic
      if ... ", one completed "a world W is deterministic if ... ".
    - The latter is problematic for those of us, like Carnap, who
      worry about mixed-mode sentences.
  - A possible worlds analysis of determinism is found in
    Earman 1986. Was it the first? 
    [[file:earman-test.jpg]]
    "The world $W\in\mathcal{W}$ is \emph{Laplacian deterministic}
    just in case for any "W'\in\mathcal{W}$, if $W$ and $W'$ agree at
    any time, then they agree for all times." (p 13)
  - Earman admits that there are pitfalls to the possible worlds
    analysis. But he thinks that it's best to give an "ontological
    formulation of an ontological doctrine".  [[file:earman2.jpg]]

    "It might be charged that the possible worlds analysis is a fraud:
    it is no more than a transcription of James' poetic vision into
    terms which are devoid of James eloquence but which display not
    much compensating gain in clarity and precision. I couldn't agree
    more! But I also think that without prejudging detailed
    substantive issues in physics we cannot do much better for a
    direct ontological formulation of what is, after all, an
    ontological doctrine." (p 14)


* Detour: What is a theory?
  - If we are going to complete "theory T is deterministic if ... ",
    then we should be more clear about what kind of thing T is.
  - Under the influence of van Fraassen, it was common to think of a
    theory as a family of models.
    - Tacitly assumed that the only -- or only interesting -- relation
      between models is identity.

* Melia
  "Call /haecceitism/ the view that there are distinct possibilities
  that differ only over which objects play which roles." (p 640)

  "Let us say that /D-haecceitism/ is the view that a theory may be
  indeterministic, even if all the different possible futures open to
  any world which makes the theory true are qualitatively identical."
  (p 640)

  "We say that a space-time theory $T$ is deterministic iff, for any
  two models $M$ and $M^*$ of $T$, any histories $S$ and $S^*$, and
  any objects $o$ and $o^*$, if there is a diffeomorphism $f:S\to S^*$
  with $f(O_i)=O^*_i$ and $f(o)=o^*$, then there exists a global
  isomorphism $g:M\to M^*$ with $g(O_i)=O^*_i$ and $g(o)=o^*$."
  (p 656)

  "A theory $T$ is deterministic iff, for any $T$-worlds $W$ and
  $W^*$, and any histories $H$ and $H^*$, if $H$ is a duplicate of
  $H^*$ and $f$ and one-to-one map from $H$ to $H^*$ which preserves
  this duplication, then $f$ can be extended to $F$, a one-to-one map
  from $W$ to $W^*$ which is, like $f$, a duplication." (p 659)

* Halvorson and Manchak's definition 

*Definition:* We say that a theory $T$ is *deterministic* just in case
  for any two models $M$ and $N$ of $T$, and initial segment
  $U\subseteq M$, if $f:M\to N$ and $g:M\to N$ are isomorphisms such
  that $f|_U=g|_U$, then $f=g$.
